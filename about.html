The author

About Me

I am an Egyptian boy who had always dreamt of magic — the kind I’d always wished I could show everyone. Writing is the closest I have come to holding it out with both hands.

By day, I work as a Project Manager and as an assistant lecturer at university. In between, I write books about train stations and cafés and sunrises, and I build websites that try to feel like places worth visiting.

The stories keep looking for the same thing: the quiet magic hiding in ordinary moments. A carriage on a line that is about to close. A café table. The first light over a shoreline. Nothing in them is far from somewhere you could actually stand — and then something shifts, and the ordinary place turns out to have been holding something all along.

I was an avid reader long before I was anything else; the shelf came well before the desk. Rick Riordan, Brandon Sanderson and Cassandra Clare are the names I would point to if you asked me where any of this started.

I am also learning Japanese — which is why the kana keep turning up here, in the titles, and in the places these stories choose to live.

All of it happens from Cairo, Egypt — the same city where, if you believe Sakura Slice Café, there is a café that was never just a café.

The through-line

What I write

Four books so far, with a fifth on its way, all circling the same few things: ordinary places, the time running quietly through them, and what it feels like to be properly seen.

Every one of them begins somewhere you could actually stand. A train carriage on a line that closes in three days. A café that was never just a café. A shoreline before dawn, where a girl is chasing a sunrise that keeps eluding her. A riverside path at sunset, and a boy listening to a tape he was never meant to hear.

Three of them are, in the end, about time. From Tomorrow is a story about time, loss, and the moments we almost miss — notes exchanged between two strangers, each dated a day the other hasn’t lived yet. The Perfect Sunrise is about memory, loss, and the quiet truth that the people we love never truly leave us, even when we can no longer see them. It says the thing plainly:

Some messages travel across time. Some sunrises change everything.

When You Were There says it harder. A grandmother’s cassette, a voice on it that turns out to be his own, and the particular cruelty of a thing that moves in only one direction — that the moments we don’t cherish are the ones we can never get back.

Sakura Slice Café takes the other road. It is a book about being seen — thirteen quiet stories about people who arrive carrying something and leave a little lighter, and about the ordinary magic of a place that asks nothing of you except that you come in, sit down, and stay as long as you need.

That is the whole of it, really. Ordinary places, turning out to hold something. Your table is already waiting for you.

Before the writing

The writers who made me want to write

Ask me why I started and I will hand you three names without hesitating: Rick Riordan, Brandon Sanderson and Cassandra Clare.

They are the reason the dream of magic never quite went away. Between them, they made an impossible thing feel like it might be standing in the next room — and that feeling is the one I have been trying to hand back ever since, one platform and one café table at a time.

So if something in these books feels familiar — a world that turns out to be a little larger than it first looked — that is where it came from.
